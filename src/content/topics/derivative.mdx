The **derivative** of a function measures how fast its output changes as its input
changes. If you know a car's position at every instant, the derivative of that position
is its speed.

The subtlety — and the reason it took mathematicians until the seventeenth century to
pin down — is that the derivative describes change at a *single instant*, and at a
single instant nothing has time to change at all.

## The idea: slope, but at a point

You already know how to measure the steepness of a straight line. Pick two points, then
divide the change in height by the change in width:

$$
\text{slope} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}
$$

A curve does not have one slope — it has a different steepness everywhere. But if you
zoom far enough into a smooth curve, the piece you are looking at becomes
indistinguishable from a straight line. The derivative is the slope of that line.

The practical version: pick your point $x$, then pick a second point a small distance
$h$ away, and measure the slope between them. As $h$ shrinks toward zero, that slope
settles down to a specific number.

<Callout type="note">
That "settles down to" is doing real work. It is the entire content of the notion of a
limit, and it is what separates a derivative from a rough approximation.
</Callout>

## The formal definition

The slope between $x$ and a nearby point $x + h$ is the **difference quotient**:

$$
\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

The derivative is what this expression approaches as $h$ goes to zero:

<Callout type="definition" title="Definition (derivative at a point)">

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

provided the limit exists. When it does, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $x$.

</Callout>

Note that we never divide by zero. We never actually *set* $h = 0$ — we ask what value
the quotient approaches as $h$ gets arbitrarily small, which is a different and
answerable question.

### Working an example by hand

Take $f(x) = x^2$. Substituting into the definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt]
      &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt]
      &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\[4pt]
      &= \lim_{h \to 0} \,(2x + h) \\[4pt]
      &= 2x
\end{aligned}
$$

The $h$ in the denominator cancels before we take the limit, which is exactly why the
definition avoids the division-by-zero problem it looks like it should have.

### Rules that save you the algebra

Doing that limit every time would be miserable, so a handful of rules cover almost
everything you will meet:

| Rule | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| Power | $\dfrac{d}{dx} x^n = n x^{n-1}$ |
| Sum | $(f + g)' = f' + g'$ |
| Product | $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$ |
| Quotient | $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ |
| Chain | $\dfrac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ |

The product rule is the one people guess wrong. The derivative of a product is *not*
the product of the derivatives — if it were, $\frac{d}{dx}(x \cdot x) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$,
but we just computed that it is $2x$.

## Where differentiability fails

Not every function has a derivative everywhere, and the failures are instructive.

$f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$, but approaching from the left gives a slope of $-1$
and from the right gives $+1$. The limit does not exist, so there is no derivative at
$0$ — a corner has no single tangent line.

Worse behaviour is possible. The **Weierstrass function** is continuous everywhere and
differentiable nowhere:

$$
W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)
$$

for suitable $a$ and $b$. When Weierstrass presented it in 1872 it was genuinely
unsettling; the prevailing intuition was that a continuous curve had to be smooth except
at isolated points. It is not: the curve is infinitely crinkled at every scale, so
zooming in never straightens it out.

<Callout type="warning">
This is why the definition insists the limit *exists*. Continuity is a strictly weaker
condition than differentiability, and the gap between them is much larger than it looks.
</Callout>

## Higher-order derivatives

The derivative of a function is itself a function, so it can be differentiated again.
The result is the **second derivative**, written $f''(x)$ or $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.

If $f$ is position, then $f'$ is velocity and $f''$ is acceleration. Continuing past
that gets you *jerk*, *snap*, *crackle*, and *pop* — genuine terms, if rarely needed
outside of engineering.

The second derivative describes curvature, which is what makes it useful for
optimisation. At a point where $f'(x) = 0$:

- $f''(x) > 0$ — the curve is concave up, so the point is a local minimum
- $f''(x) < 0$ — the curve is concave down, so the point is a local maximum
- $f''(x) = 0$ — inconclusive; $f(x) = x^4$ and $f(x) = x^3$ both land here at the
  origin, and only one of them has a minimum there

Taken to its limit, this idea gives the **Taylor series**: if you know every derivative
of a function at a single point, you can often reconstruct the entire function
everywhere. That reconstruction is the engine behind
[Euler's identity](/topics/euler-identity/).
