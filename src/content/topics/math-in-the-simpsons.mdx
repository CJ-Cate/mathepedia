*The Simpsons* has, at various points, employed a writing staff with a density of
advanced mathematics degrees that would be respectable in a university department. The
result is two decades of jokes written for an audience the writers knew would be
vanishingly small.

The best of them are not jokes *about* mathematics. They are jokes that require you to
do mathematics to notice they were there.

## The writers' room

The unusual staffing was largely a hiring accident that compounded. **Al Jean**, a
showrunner across much of the series, entered Harvard at sixteen to study mathematics.
**Ken Keeler** holds a PhD in applied mathematics from Harvard. **David X. Cohen** studied
physics at Harvard and took a master's in computer science at Berkeley. **Jeff
Westbrook** left a computer science research post at Yale to write television.

They mostly wrote ordinary comedy. But the freeze-frame gag — a blackboard visible for a
second and a half — turned out to be a convenient place to leave things for whoever was
paying enough attention, and in the pre-streaming era that meant whoever owned a VCR and
was willing to use the pause button.

The tradition continued into *Futurama*, where Keeler proved a genuinely new theorem
about permutation groups for an episode plot. The **Futurama theorem** was written for a
1999 body-swapping story, and it is a real result — published nowhere except a cartoon,
and cited since in actual group theory literature.

## The Fermat near-misses

The most carefully constructed gags are two equations that appear to disprove
**Fermat's Last Theorem**.

<Callout type="theorem" title="Fermat's Last Theorem">

No three positive integers $a$, $b$, $c$ satisfy

$$
a^n + b^n = c^n
$$

for any integer $n > 2$.

Conjectured by Pierre de Fermat in 1637, proved by Andrew Wiles in 1994.

</Callout>

In "Treehouse of Horror VI" (1995), as Homer drifts through a three-dimensional void, an
equation floats past:

$$
1782^{12} + 1841^{12} = 1922^{12}
$$

Three years later, in "The Wizard of Evergreen Terrace" (1998), a blackboard behind Homer
reads:

$$
3987^{12} + 4365^{12} = 4472^{12}
$$

Both are false — Wiles had already proved that no such equation can be true. What makes
them worth the trouble is *how* false they are.

### Built to defeat a calculator

David X. Cohen wrote a program to search for near-misses specifically, and the search
criterion was not "close." It was "close enough to survive the check a viewer would
actually run."

Take the second equation. The two sides are:

```
3987^12 + 4365^12 = 63976656349698612616236230953154487896987106
4472^12           = 63976656348486725806862358322168575784124416
```

They differ — by about $1.2 \times 10^{33}$, which is an enormous number. But both sides
have 44 digits, and they agree on the first **ten**.

That number is not a coincidence. A [TI-84 Plus](/topics/ti-84-plus/) displays ten
significant digits. A viewer in 1998 who paused the tape, typed both sides into the
calculator on their desk, and compared the results would have seen two identical numbers
and concluded that Fermat's Last Theorem had just been disproved on network television.

The first equation is tuned the same way: 40 digits, agreeing on the first ten.

### Two ways to catch it without a bigger calculator

You do not need arbitrary-precision arithmetic to see that these are false. You need to
stop comparing magnitudes and start comparing residues.

**The 1995 equation falls to parity.** $1782$ is even and $1841$ is odd, so:

$$
\underbrace{1782^{12}}_{\text{even}} + \underbrace{1841^{12}}_{\text{odd}} = \text{odd}
$$

But $1922$ is even, so $1922^{12}$ is even. An odd number cannot equal an even one. The
equation dies in one line, and Cohen has acknowledged the oversight — the second attempt
fixed it, choosing $3987$ and $4365$ both odd so the sum is even, matching the even
$4472^{12}$.

**The 1998 equation falls modulo 9.** A number is congruent mod $9$ to the sum of its
digits, which makes this checkable by hand:

- $3 + 9 + 8 + 7 = 27$, so $3987 \equiv 0 \pmod 9$
- $4 + 3 + 6 + 5 = 18$, so $4365 \equiv 0 \pmod 9$
- $4 + 4 + 7 + 2 = 17 \to 1 + 7 = 8$, so $4472 \equiv -1 \pmod 9$

Therefore the left side is $0^{12} + 0^{12} \equiv 0$, while the right side is
$(-1)^{12} \equiv 1$. Zero is not one.

<Callout type="note">
This is the general lesson these gags are built around: agreement in the leading digits
is not evidence of equality. Two numbers can match to ten places, a hundred places, or a
million, and still differ. Numerical agreement is a hint to go looking for a proof, never
a substitute for one.
</Callout>

## The rest of the blackboard

The Fermat equations are the famous ones, but they share their frames with a lot else.

The same 1995 sequence that carries $1782^{12} + 1841^{12} = 1922^{12}$ also displays
**P = NP** — a claim about computational complexity that remains, three decades later,
one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems and worth a million dollars to whoever settles
it. [Euler's identity](/topics/euler-identity/) appears in the same void.

The 1998 blackboard is the densest. Alongside the Fermat near-miss, Homer works through:

- A calculation of the mass of the Higgs boson, produced roughly fourteen years before
  CERN measured it. The estimate was in the right region.
- A topological transformation of a doughnut into a sphere — a joke on Homer's
  established relationship with doughnuts, and on the fact that this transformation is
  precisely what topology says you *cannot* do. A torus has one hole and a sphere has
  none, and no continuous deformation changes that. The blackboard shows him doing it
  anyway.
- An expression whose terms sum to a prediction about the universe being closed rather
  than open.

Elsewhere the register drops. "Bart the Genius" (1990) turns on a calculus joke —
$\frac{dy}{dr}$ integrated to give $r\,dr\,r$, read aloud as "har de har har." "MoneyBART"
(2010) puts Lisa in charge of a Little League team armed with sabermetrics, and hangs a
Fermat reference on the outfield wall.

None of this was ever going to land with a general audience. That is more or less the
point: the gags were left in for the fraction of viewers equipped to find them, and the
show carried on without waiting for anyone to catch up.
