The **TI-84 Plus** is a graphing calculator that has remained in continuous production
since 2004, largely unchanged, and remains standard equipment in American secondary
mathematics classrooms.

Its processor is a Zilog Z80 — the same chip that ran the Sinclair ZX Spectrum in 1982.
A modern phone outruns it by roughly five orders of magnitude. It has survived not on
technical merit but on institutional inertia: it is permitted on the SAT, ACT, and AP
exams, and no competitor has managed to dislodge it from that approval list.

## Getting started

The interface has not changed meaningfully in twenty years, so instructions written in
2006 still apply.

**Graphing a function.** Press `Y=`, type an expression using the `X,T,θ,n` key for the
variable, then press `GRAPH`. If nothing appears, the viewing window is wrong — press
`ZOOM` `6` for the standard $[-10, 10]$ window, or `ZOOM` `0` for ZoomFit.

**Finding a specific value.** `2nd` `TRACE` opens the CALC menu:

| Option | Finds |
| --- | --- |
| `1: value` | $f(x)$ at a chosen $x$ |
| `2: zero` | A root, within a bracket you specify |
| `3: minimum` / `4: maximum` | A local extremum in a bracket |
| `5: intersect` | Where two plotted curves cross |
| `6: dy/dx` | The numerical [derivative](/topics/derivative/) at a point |
| `7: ∫f(x)dx` | A definite integral over an interval |

Options 2 through 5 ask for a left bound, a right bound, and a guess. This trips up
nearly every new user, but it is not arbitrary — the calculator is running a numerical
root-finder that needs a bracket to search inside.

**The most common mistake.** The `(-)` key and the `−` key are different. `(-)` is
negation, `−` is subtraction. Using the wrong one produces `ERR: SYNTAX` with no
explanation of what went wrong.

<Callout type="note">
`2nd` `MODE` quits back to the home screen from almost anywhere. `2nd` `+` reaches the
memory menu, where `7: Reset` restores factory defaults — useful when a borrowed
calculator has been left in Degree mode or with a bizarre window.
</Callout>

## What it computes, and how

Everything the TI-84 reports is a numerical approximation. It has no computer algebra
system — it cannot manipulate symbols, only numbers. Knowing which algorithm sits behind
each menu item explains most of its surprising answers.

### Numerical differentiation

`nDeriv(` does not differentiate symbolically. It evaluates the **symmetric difference
quotient**:

$$
f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}
$$

with $h = 10^{-3}$ by default. This is more accurate than the one-sided version — its
error is $O(h^2)$ rather than $O(h)$ — but it has a specific and well-known failure
mode. Ask it for the derivative of $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$:

$$
\frac{|0 + h| - |0 - h|}{2h} = \frac{h - h}{2h} = 0
$$

The calculator confidently reports $0$. The true derivative does not exist there; the
symmetric formula averages the slopes on either side of the corner and returns a number
that means nothing.

<Callout type="warning">
The TI-84 will not tell you a derivative fails to exist. It reports a number regardless.
Check for corners and discontinuities yourself before trusting `nDeriv(` output.
</Callout>

### Numerical integration

`fnInt(` uses **Gauss–Kronrod quadrature**, an adaptive method that samples the integrand
at strategically chosen points and estimates its own error. It is accurate for smooth
integrands and struggles with the things adaptive quadrature always struggles with:
integrable singularities, wildly oscillating integrands, and infinite intervals faked
with a large finite bound.

### Floating-point representation

The TI-84 stores numbers in binary-coded decimal with 14 significant digits internally,
displaying 10. This gives it better behaviour than binary floating point on decimal
arithmetic — `0.1 + 0.2` gives exactly `0.3`, where IEEE 754 double precision famously
does not.

It does not escape floating-point error, only relocates it. Subtracting nearly equal
numbers still destroys precision:

$$
\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} \quad \text{evaluated at } x = 10^{-6}
$$

should be very close to $0.5$. The calculator returns $0$, because $\cos(10^{-6})$
rounds to exactly $1$ at 14 digits, and the numerator collapses to zero before the
division happens. This is **catastrophic cancellation**, and it is a property of finite
precision, not a bug in the device.

## Programming it

The TI-84 ships with **TI-BASIC**, an interpreted language reached through `PRGM`
`NEW`. It is slow — a Z80 interpreting tokenised BASIC — but it is genuinely
programmable, and for a great many students it is the first programming environment they
ever touch.

```
PROGRAM:QUAD
:Prompt A,B,C
:B²-4AC→D
:If D<0
:Then
:Disp "NO REAL ROOTS"
:Else
:Disp (-B+√(D))/(2A)
:Disp (-B-√(D))/(2A)
:End
```

For anything performance-sensitive, the alternative is Z80 assembly, which runs on the
order of a hundred times faster. A substantial homebrew scene has built games, shells,
and utilities this way for two decades.

<Callout type="warning">
Exam boards require memory to be cleared before standardised tests, and proctors do
check. Anything you write can and will be wiped — keep a backup on a computer via
TI Connect.
</Callout>

## Why it still costs what it does

The TI-84 Plus has held a price around 100–150 USD for two decades while its component
cost fell to a few dollars. The economics are unusual and worth understanding.

The purchasing decision is made by school districts and exam boards, not by the students
who pay. Texas Instruments supplies teacher training, lesson plans, and classroom sets;
the curriculum then specifies keystroke sequences that only work on TI hardware. A
student cannot substitute a cheaper or better calculator without doing extra work to
translate every instruction they are given.

Free alternatives — [Desmos](https://www.desmos.com/) in particular — are
comprehensively better as graphing tools, and Desmos is now the built-in calculator for
several state standardised tests. But a phone or laptop cannot be brought into an exam
room, and a dedicated device that cannot communicate can. That constraint, more than any
feature, is what the price actually buys.
